The Plastic Surgeon and the Spy: Buying Time

The Influence of Life Experiences on Our Perceptions of Truth

When I began writing The Plastic Surgeon and the Spy: Buying Time, I never set out to compare medicine with espionage. The contrast emerged as I developed the characters and their worlds, and it became one of the most compelling aspects of the story.

Two Professions, Two Assumptions About Truth

Doctors work in an environment built on trust. Physicians and patients ordinarily share the same goal: health and well-being. Patients need to disclose details that may be embarrassing, uncomfortable, or difficult to put into words, because the doctor's decisions, and the patient's welfare, depend on an accurate picture.

Law enforcement and intelligence agencies operate in a very different environment. People under investigation may have incentives to lie, conceal, and mislead because honesty can have consequences for them. The roles can be adversarial, and the incentives may point in opposite directions. Most people, of course, fall somewhere between these extremes, with varying degrees of honesty and dishonesty.

Over time, people can become conditioned by their roles. Investigators may view others with suspicion, while medical professionals tend to believe what they are told. This is not necessarily naivety; it reflects what each profession needs in order to function.

Neither picture is absolute. Patients do not always tell the whole truth, and medicine itself is an imperfect science, where diagnosis often rests on probability rather than certainty. There are also situations in which physicians must learn to recognize deliberate deception and seek corroborating evidence rather than relying solely on a patient's account.

Yet the core purpose of medicine remains clear. The doctor seeks to identify the problem and choose the best course of action. Espionage operates on different assumptions. Information may be withheld, identities concealed, and statements crafted to mislead. Someone who appears helpful may have an entirely different agenda. The challenge is not only to discover what is happening, but to judge whether the information behind a decision can be trusted.

I came to see this distinction most clearly through two characters, Jack Lambert and Emily Chan.

Jack: The Search for Truth

Jack is a physician trained to think scientifically. He observes, gathers evidence, tests assumptions, and draws conclusions from facts. As a surgeon, he knows his decisions have tangible consequences.

He also knows the limits of his field. Scientific knowledge is finite, which is why patients can legitimately seek second opinions. Medicine is never fully predictable, although outcomes can often be assessed and measured.

Jack tends to trust people. His career has taught him to listen, take others seriously, and act in their best interests. These are admirable qualities in a doctor, but they become vulnerabilities in a world where deception is deliberate.

His difficulty is not a lack of intelligence or judgment. He is entering unfamiliar territory where the habits that have served him so well may no longer be enough, and he is somewhat out of his depth. He must learn that his own sincerity does not guarantee honesty in others, and that trusting someone is not the same as knowing they can be trusted.

I recognize part of myself in Jack. I have sometimes struggled to tell honesty from deception, and I have experienced the consequences of trusting people who abused that trust. Jack's vulnerability is, in part, my own.

Emily: The Power of Perception

Emily sees the world differently. She is intelligent, shrewd, and gifted at understanding and influencing people. She recognizes that relationships are shaped not only by facts but also by appearance, attraction, emotion, and the expectations people bring to every encounter.

Her decision to undergo cosmetic surgery reflects this. It is driven by more than a professional interest in her own appearance. She understands the power of physical attractiveness and deliberately uses her sexuality as a tool to achieve her goals. She pairs that awareness with a talent for reading people and adapting her behavior to the situation.

Where Jack seeks evidence to uncover the truth, Emily understands how perception can be shaped. She is at home in the uncertain realm of human motives, where conclusions cannot be tested as neatly as they can in medicine.

She is also a natural manipulator who does not draw the same clear lines between right and wrong that Jack does. She can feel empathy and recognize injustice, yet she has few moral qualms about bending the truth if she believes the cause is worthwhile.

None of this means Emily lacks a conscience. Her moral framework simply differs from Jack's. She is more inclined to judge an action by its purpose than by whether the method itself is ethically acceptable. To her, a noble objective can justify methods Jack would find objectionable.

There is a quiet irony in Emily's choice. Cosmetic surgery is not inherently deceptive. The change is real, visible, and freely chosen. But appearance is also a signal, and people draw conclusions from it about youth, health, and character. Jack's profession is grounded in evidence and honest disclosure, yet his specialty deals in the very surface impressions that Emily understands so well. If surgery is used to deceive, the deception lies in the patient's choices, not the surgeon's. Jack practices in good faith, but good faith leaves him exposed to a patient who sees his skills as a means to an end.

Neither character is infallible. Jack's integrity does not protect him from manipulation, and Emily's resourcefulness does not guarantee that her judgments are correct. Their contrasting strengths and weaknesses give their relationship its complexity and tension.

Beyond the World of the Novel

The questions Jack and Emily raise extend well beyond medicine and espionage. They matter wherever decisions are shaped by competing interests, beliefs, and ambitions. In politics and international relations, evidence alone rarely settles a dispute. People may interpret the same facts differently, favor information that confirms what they already believe, or place national interest above conclusions that might otherwise be persuasive.

Scientific reasoning helps us separate evidence from assumption, but it cannot eliminate human motives. Facts cannot tell us what people value or what they are prepared to do. Understanding the world therefore requires more than establishing what is true. It also requires understanding why people believe what they believe, what they hope to achieve, and how far they will go to achieve it.

Trust, Truth, and the Human Condition

The contrast between Jack and Emily raises questions that reach into everyday life. How do we know when someone is telling the truth? How much of our judgment rests on verification, and how much on trust in another person? What happens when someone exploits our willingness to trust for their own ends?

These questions are hard because human behavior is less predictable than we would like. We can weigh evidence, consider motives, and learn from experience, yet we can never fully see into another person's mind. Intelligence and experience offer no absolute protection against deception.

Honesty is not the same as good judgment, just as skill at manipulation is not the same as wisdom. Jack and Emily represent two ways of navigating uncertainty, each with its own strengths and limitations.

Writing this novel allowed me to explore these differences through characters whose professions, temperaments, and moral standards lead them to engage with the world from very different, and often opposing, perspectives.

I began with a story and found, along the way, a deeper question at its heart: How do we hold on to our principles and our judgment in a world where the truth cannot always be taken at face value?

I will leave the rest for you to discover.
